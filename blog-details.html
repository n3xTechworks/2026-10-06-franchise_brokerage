7 Due-Diligence Checks Before You Buy a Small Business

October 8, 2026 Daniel Whitmore 7 min read

Buying an established business can shorten the path to profit by years, but only if the business is what the seller says it is. Due diligence is the structured process of checking those claims before you are legally committed. Most deals that go wrong did so because a buyer skipped a step or accepted a verbal answer. The seven checks below are the ones we walk every client through, in roughly the order we recommend doing them.

1. Verify the financials

Start with the numbers, because everything else is priced from them. Ask for three years of federal tax returns, profit and loss statements and balance sheets, then reconcile them against bank statements and sales-tax filings. The figures should tell the same story.

  • Compare reported revenue with actual bank deposits for at least 12 months.
  • Question every add-back. Owner perks are legitimate; “one-off” expenses that recur each year are not.
  • Look for seasonality, falling margins and a sudden jump in sales just before the listing date.

2. Test customer and revenue quality

A business with one customer worth 40% of sales is a very different asset from one with 400 customers worth 0.25% each. Ask for a customer concentration report and check how much revenue is recurring versus one-off. If possible, speak with the top five customers after the letter of intent is signed.

“Price is what you pay; cash flow is what you buy. Due diligence is how you confirm the cash flow is real.”

3. Review contracts, leases and legal standing

Many small businesses depend on documents that do not automatically transfer to a new owner. Ask your attorney to review the lease, supplier agreements, franchise agreement (if any), software licences and any pending or past litigation.

  • Lease: remaining term, renewal options, rent escalations and whether the landlord must approve an assignment.
  • Licences and permits: confirm they are current and transferable.
  • Liens and debts: run a UCC search so you do not inherit secured creditors.

4. Inspect assets, inventory and operations

Walk the premises with a checklist. Confirm that equipment listed in the sale actually exists, is in working order and is owned outright. Count inventory on the day of closing rather than relying on a figure from months earlier, and ask how the business really runs day to day: who opens, who orders stock, who handles complaints. Documented systems are a sign of a transferable business.

5. Understand the team and the owner’s role

Find out how much of the business lives in the owner’s head. If key staff, the owner’s relationships or a specialist licence drive the results, negotiate a transition period of 60 to 90 days, and consider retention bonuses for essential employees. Review payroll records, employment contracts and any outstanding wage or benefit obligations.

6. Check market position and growth potential

Due diligence is not only about risk. Look at local competitors, online reviews, pricing trends and the industry outlook. Ask whether growth has been limited by capacity, marketing or the owner’s appetite, because those constraints are the opportunities you are paying for.

7. Plan financing and closing conditions

Finally, make sure the deal can close. Confirm lender requirements early, since SBA lenders will want their own verification of earnings, and write protections into the purchase agreement: representations and warranties, an inventory adjustment, a non-compete for the seller and, where appropriate, an escrow holdback.

Final thoughts

Thorough due diligence typically takes 30 to 60 days and costs a small fraction of the purchase price. It either confirms that you are buying a sound business or gives you the evidence to renegotiate or walk away. Either outcome is a good one.
